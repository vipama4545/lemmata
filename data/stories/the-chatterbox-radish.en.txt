The Chatterbox Radish

“Ah, about the potato. Truly they say: ‘It is man’s friend… a good, useful plant!’” said the Sunflower.

At that moment, the potato was blooming with rose and light-blue flowers. Some of them had already turned into small green balls. All the other greens leaned toward the potato, making it feel bashful.

“I do not have such grand ancestors as the radish,” the potato said quietly. “However, all my ancestors strived for only one thing: to serve and bring benefit to the people. They ate boiled, fried, and baked potato; they made starch from it and distilled vodka… I remember, in one village, they ran out of bread, and my brothers fed the poor all winter long, saving many from death.”

The cucumber spread its vines across the garden bed, brushed past the potato with its tendrils, and looked at it for a long time, feeling sad that it could not bring so much benefit itself.

“Tell me, what do they eat of yours? Those round little balloons?” Poppy asked the potato.

“No, no. Deep in the soil, I have thin roots, and on these roots sit thick tubers or knobs; these tubers have tiny black eyes. These are what they call potatoes, which humans eat.”

The potato smiled gently.

“No, I cannot see. Those eyes are tiny hollows where seeds are born. You can cut a potato into pieces, plant the parts with eyes into the soil, and potatoes like me will grow.”

“Remarkable! They usually sow our seeds,” noted the Sunflower.

“Me too!” exclaimed the radish. “Though you will ripen this very year, while I will only ripen next year—because I am a two-year plant.”

“What do you mean by that?” asked the Sunflower.

“By my mother’s soul…” the radish began again. But it shook its head and looked toward the potato.

“Then why did those green pods grow from your flowers?” the potato asked the radish.

“That is my fruit! It is called a multi-seeded fruit, and the seeds ripen inside it. It can also be sown.”

“My seed is a fruit too!” said the cucumber.

“My fruit is a pod.”

“Funny fruits!” said the cabbage.

“My fruit is a two-seeded pod,” declared the carrot.

“And mine is a capsule,” said the Poppy. “At first it is bluish, but later it dries up and turns chestnut brown. Inside, the capsule is divided into four sections; when the seed ripens, tiny holes open at the top. When the wind blows, it sways and scatters the seeds in all directions.”

Everyone listened attentively to Poppy’s talk.

“My fruit is called a basket,” said the Sunflower.

“It really does look like a basket,” a high-pitched voice called out from the bottom of the fence. “Just look at my fruit; it can fly with the help of the wind!”

“Who is that?” Sunflower asked Poppy.

“That is Dandelion grass,” Poppy answered.

“Move over, Poppy, move! Get out of the way! Stop shaking your red head, you’re bothering me!...” shouted the radish. “When the mistress arrives, I’ll tell on you for not giving me peace! By my mother’s soul, everyone knows my grandmother was…”

“We heard you, we heard you!” all the greens shouted in unison, and at last, they didn’t let the radish speak any further.

Autumn arrived. In our familiar garden, it was harvest time. Every day, the grandmother would come to the garden with her grandchildren and a maidservant to gather the vegetables. It had been a long time since they picked the cucumbers and pickled them with blackcurrant leaves. The pickled cucumbers turned out wonderful—they were firm and green! But the cucumber that hated fighting in the garden lay yellow and thick on the bed, ripening for seed. It had also been a long time since the potatoes were dug up; they ate them tossed with clarified butter, praising them: “Ooh, what delicious, large potatoes!”

They simmered cabbage soup and baked pies. Chickpeas, carrots, and turnips constantly crunched in the teeth of the children, who loved to treat their sweet tooth. Grandmother gathered the poppy seeds and was surely going to bake sweet cakes for her grandchildren.

The swollen radish lay quietly on the bed. Now it no longer acted bossy; it looked around grandly, waiting to be taken to an exhibition so it could win fame…

One day, grandmother came to the garden with her grandchildren, bringing a hired woman along with her.

“It’s time to dig up the radish too. Come on, Khristina, get to work!” said the grandmother. The radish puffed up even more and whispered: “Now you will find out who I am… You’ll see what fine stock I come from. You’ll be grateful you grew alongside me in this garden.”

“Look, Grandma, what a huge radish! What a radish!” exclaimed a young girl, pointing to our chatterbox radish.

“It’s big, but it’s useless!” grandmother said with a smile.

“Can we pull it up?” asked a grandchild.

“Pull it up, my dear!”

With laughter and jokes, the children pulled out the large radish.

“They’re digging up a grand radish!... They’re pulling up the grandmother-radish!”

“Look, look…” whispered the carrots, turnips, beets, and potatoes, leaning forward out of curiosity to see what was happening.

“Grandma, dear! This giant radish turned out to be hollow!” said the young boy, holding the radish up high.

Every single vegetable saw that the radish was indeed hollow.

“That’s why it was such a chatterbox,” Celery whispered to the cabbage.

“Ma’am, what should I do with this?” asked the hired woman.

“Throw it far away,” grandmother replied.

“Wait… my great-grandmother…” the shamed radish began. But just then, it was tossed over the fence, and it fell right into the ditch.

Author: Cordeli.
